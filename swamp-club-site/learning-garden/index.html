How I Learn Design

Masters of Graphic Design

This is one corner of the swamp where I keep my teachers. The way I learn design is simple — I go to the source and study the people who shaped how the modern page looks. Thirty-one designers across seven traditions, each redrawn by hand so I actually understand the decision underneath the work, not just admire it.

By Sophie · sophieren.comSeven traditions · Thirty-one practitioners · More notes growing →
01

Swiss Style & Grid Systems

The rational backbone of editorial design — objective, structured, built on the mathematics of the grid. The tradition I lean on most when I want order before expression.

01 · ZÜRICH

Josef Müller-Brockmann

1914–1996 · Switzerland

Father of the grid system.

His book Grid Systems in Graphic Design codified the modular grid as a discipline. Concert posters built from pure geometry, rhythm and Akzidenz-Grotesk — no decoration, only structure.

Take: Structure is the message

02 · NEW YORK / MILAN

Massimo Vignelli

1931–2014 · Italy / USA

“If you can design one thing, you can design everything.”

The NYC Subway map, the American Airlines identity, the National Park guides. A radical minimalist working from a handful of typefaces — Helvetica, Bodoni, Garamond — who refused fashion.

Take: Less, but better — forever

03 · AMSTERDAM

Wim Crouwel

1928–2019 · Netherlands

“Mr. Gridnik.”

Designer of the experimental New Alphabet and decades of Stedelijk Museum catalogues. Pushed systematic, grid-locked typography to its most extreme, machine-logical end.

Take: Total systems thinking

04 · LEIPZIG / BASEL

Jan Tschichold

1902–1974 · Germany / Switzerland

Author of Die neue Typographie.

Codified asymmetric modernist typography, then later wrote the Penguin Books composition rules that disciplined an entire paperback empire. A one-man bridge from the avant-garde to everyday print.

Take: Rules set the type free

05 · SWITZERLAND / PARIS

Adrian Frutiger

1928–2015 · Switzerland

Designer of Univers & Frutiger.

Mapped the first truly systematic type family — Univers, organised by a numbered weight grid — and drew the signage face for Charles de Gaulle airport that became a global standard for legibility.

Take: A typeface is a system, not a shape

02

Magazine & Editorial Design

The art of pacing a reader through pages — white space, scale, contrast, and the choreography of the turning page. The tradition I steal from every time I build a page on my own site.

06 · NEW YORK

Alexey Brodovitch

1898–1971 · Russia / USA

Legendary art director of Harper's Bazaar.

Over 24 years he defined modern magazine rhythm — dramatic white space, full-bleed photography, type that breathes across the spread. His mantra to students: “Astonish me.”

Take: The spread is the unit, not the page

07 · NEW YORK / PARIS

Fabien Baron

b. 1959 · France

Harper's Bazaar, Interview, Calvin Klein.

The most influential editorial director of the 1990s. Oversized Didot type, stark black-and-white, luxurious emptiness — a contemporary minimal elegance that still defines fashion publishing.

Take: Confidence is built from emptiness

08 · LONDON

Neville Brody

b. 1957 · United Kingdom

Art director of The Face.

Turned 1980s magazine typography into raw, expressive, rule-breaking art. Custom letterforms and bold graphic systems made the page itself feel like youth culture.

Take: Type can be attitude

09 · NEW YORK

Cipe Pineles

1908–1991 · Austria / USA

First autonomous female art director in the US.

Art-directed Seventeen, Charm and Glamour, commissioning fine artists to illustrate editorial and treating young women readers as intelligent. The first woman admitted to the New York Art Directors Club.

Take: Respect the reader and the page follows

10 · NEW YORK

George Lois

1931–2022 · USA

The Esquire covers.

Ninety-two covers that turned a magazine into cultural commentary — Ali pierced by arrows, Warhol drowning in a soup can. Proof that one fearless image can say more than the whole issue.

Take: One image, maximum nerve

03

Typography, Posters & Identity

Designers who made type and mark do the heavy lifting — from corporate identity to type-as-image. The discipline of saying the most with a single sign.

11 · NEW YORK

Paula Scher

b. 1948 · USA · Pentagram

Type as image, environment as canvas.

The Public Theater identity, Citibank, the typographic maps. Bold, dense, energetic lettering that fills the whole frame and turns words into pictures.

Take: Words can be the image

12 · LOS ANGELES

Saul Bass

1920–1996 · USA

Film titles & corporate marks.

Title sequences for Hitchcock and Scorsese; logos for AT&T and United Airlines. Reduced complex ideas to a single, unforgettable graphic gesture.

Take: One idea, distilled to a symbol

13 · NEW YORK

Paul Rand

1914–1996 · USA

Father of corporate identity.

IBM, ABC, UPS, Westinghouse. Brought European modernism to American business and proved a logo could be witty, simple, and built to last for decades.

Take: Simplicity is hard-won, not plain

14 · NEW YORK

Sagmeister & Walsh

Stefan b. 1962 · Jessica b. 1986

Conceptual, provocative, hand-made.

Album art, TED-stage projects and identity work that treats emotion and risk as raw material. Proof that systematic discipline and wild concept can share a studio.

Take: An idea you can feel

04

Posters & Cultural Landmarks

Work that escaped the design world and entered the culture — images so strong they became part of the shared visual language.

15 · NEW YORK

Milton Glaser

1929–2020 · USA

“I♥NY” and the Dylan poster.

Co-founder of New York magazine and Push Pin Studios. Warm, eclectic, idea-driven work that bridged fine art and popular communication.

Take: An idea beats a style

16 · BASEL

Armin Hofmann

1920–2020 · Switzerland

The Basel School poster tradition.

Master teacher whose posters balanced point, line and plane with near-musical precision. Shaped generations through the Basel design pedagogy.

Take: Contrast, reduced to essentials

17 · NEW YORK

Herb Lubalin

1918–1981 · USA

Typographic genius; co-creator of Avant Garde.

Pioneered “typographics” — letterforms ligatured, overlapped and sculpted into images. The Mother & Child mark and the magazine Avant Garde defined an era of type.

Take: Letters are a material to shape

18 · MOSCOW

El Lissitzky

1890–1941 · Russia

Constructivist pioneer.

“Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge.” Fused art, typography and politics into dynamic geometric compositions that prefigured the entire modernist project.

Take: Geometry as force and direction

19 · PARIS

A. M. Cassandre

1901–1968 · Ukraine / France

Art Deco poster master.

Posters for the ocean liner Normandie, Dubonnet and the Nord Express — monumental geometry, airbrushed light and integrated lettering that made advertising look like modern art.

Take: Scale and geometry as spectacle

20 · LOS ANGELES

Shepard Fairey

b. 1970 · USA

OBEY & the Obama Hope poster.

From a skate-sticker campaign to one of the most reproduced political images ever made. Stencilled propaganda aesthetics turned into street-level mass communication.

Take: A poster can still move millions

05

Digital, New Wave & Postmodern

When the Macintosh arrived, a generation tore up the grid — pixels, layers, noise and type that argues with itself. The tradition closest to the swamp's own remix instinct.

21 · LOS ANGELES

April Greiman

b. 1948 · USA

Pioneer of digital design.

One of the first designers to treat the Macintosh as a real medium — layering video stills, pixels and type into the hybrid imagery that defined American New Wave. Her Design Quarterly 133 was a single fold-out poster of herself, life-size, built from pixels.

Take: The computer is a material

22 · SAN DIEGO

David Carson

b. 1954 · USA

Art director of Ray Gun.

The face of 1990s deconstructed typography — torn grids, illegible-on-purpose layouts, type as raw emotion. He once set a dull interview entirely in a dingbat font. "The end of print," they called it.

Take: Break it until it feels something

23 · BERKELEY

Licko & VanderLans

Zuzana b. 1961 · Rudy b. 1955

Founders of Emigre.

Their magazine and type foundry made the low-resolution screen a virtue — Licko's bitmap faces embraced the pixel instead of hiding it, and Emigre became the laboratory where digital typography grew up.

Take: Design with the grain of the machine

06

Japanese & Asian Masters

A whole parallel lineage the Western canon usually skips — where Swiss rationality met Japanese space, calligraphy and emptiness, and produced some of the strongest graphic design on earth.

24 · TOKYO

Yusaku Kamekura

1915–1997 · Japan

The Tokyo 1964 Olympics identity.

The godfather of modern Japanese graphic design. His rising-sun emblem and photographic posters for the 1964 Games fused European modernism with a distinctly Japanese sense of symbol and ceremony.

Take: A nation can be one mark

25 · TOKYO

Ikko Tanaka

1930–2002 · Japan

Geometric tradition; creative force behind MUJI.

Built faces of Noh and kabuki actors out of pure geometric colour blocks, and shaped the early art direction of MUJI. The cleanest possible meeting of grid logic and Japanese theatre.

Take: Tradition, rebuilt from rectangles

26 · TOKYO

Tadanori Yokoo

b. 1936 · Japan

Japan's psychedelic provocateur.

The anti-Swiss — lurid rising suns, collage, eros and pop, often compared to Warhol but rooted in Japanese print. Proof that a national design culture is big enough to contain its own opposite.

Take: Order needs its wild twin

27 · TOKYO

Kenya Hara

b. 1958 · Japan

Art director of MUJI; theorist of emptiness.

Built a whole design philosophy around white, void and the un-designed. His MUJI horizon posters say almost nothing on purpose — and let the viewer pour everything in.

Take: Emptiness is the loudest space

28 · HONG KONG

Kan Tai-keung

b. 1942 · China / Hong Kong

Ink painting meets modern identity.

A founding figure of modern Chinese graphic design, fusing traditional ink-brush gesture and the seal with Western systems — proof the grid can hold a thousand years of calligraphy.

Take: Heritage as living material

07

Latin American Masters

Latin America took the imported grid and gave it a wholly new voice — Ulm-trained rigour, revolutionary silkscreen posters, an Olympic identity that became a landmark. Design as identity, and often as resistance.

29 · MEXICO CITY

Lance Wyman

b. 1937 · USA / Mexico

The Mexico '68 Olympic identity.

American-born, but his system for the 1968 Mexico City Games is the defining landmark of Latin American design identity — radiating Op-Art letterforms fused with pre-Hispanic pattern, and a pictogram language that set the world standard.

Take: A place can author a style

30 · SÃO PAULO

Alexandre Wollner

1928–2018 · Brazil

Father of Brazilian visual design.

Ulm-trained, he brought rigorous grid modernism home and built the foundations of Brazilian corporate identity — including the Banco Itaú system. Concrete-art logic, tropical clarity.

Take: Import the method, grow your own voice

31 · HAVANA

Félix Beltrán

1938–2022 · Cuba / Mexico

Father of the Cuban poster.

His post-1959 political posters turned flat colour and bold geometry into a revolutionary visual language seen around the world — silkscreen urgency with a designer's precision.

Take: A poster is a position

FIELD NOTE · WHY I COPY FIRST

Copying the masters before I trust my own taste

I don't learn design by collecting screenshots. I learn it by redrawing the work until I understand the decision underneath it — why the grid lands where it does, why the white space is that big, why one red wedge can carry a whole revolution. Every sketch on this page is something I traced by hand so the idea would stick.

Seven traditions, thirty-one people. Some teach me order, some teach me nerve. I keep them in the same swamp on purpose — because the work I want to make lives exactly where Swiss discipline meets a little swamp-club mischief.

— Sophie · Field Notes N°01 · more growing in the swamp
